# Making the humeter world readable: what the books do (as of 2026-09-02)

Sources read at page level (renders under /tmp/books/*/pages, agent notes in /tmp/books/*/observations.md):
Hennig, Rediscovering the World (2013); Dorling, Newman and Barford, The Atlas of the Real World (2008);
Phaidon, Map: Exploring the World (2015); Jacobs, Strange Maps (2009); Kriz et al., Understanding Different
Geographies (2013). Verified: none of them uses optimal transport; Hennig is Gastner-Newman diffusion throughout
(ArcGIS/ScapeToad, pp. 96-97), gridded at 1/4 degree globally, 2.5' per country, 100 m for London (p. 92).

## The rules the books agree on

1. Never show a cartogram alone. Hennig's triptych (gridded cartogram / conventional cartogram / reference map,
   Fig. 4.1 p. 113); a ghost of the untransformed shape behind the plates; a thumbnail reference inset with the
   same colours (Figs. 6.4-6.5 pp. 184-185). The Atlas puts the base map first (001 Land Area, p. 20) and
   tells the reader to go back to it (p. 14). Strange Maps: the "original map" thumbnail on every panel (p. 190).
2. Colour is identity, not value. Twelve regional hues, countries within a region differ by lightness only,
   the same colour on every map (Atlas p. 17). Hennig p. 143: familiar shapes disappear, "the use of a
   consistent colour scheme and the comparison to a conventional reference map however helps". His London
   100 m answer to abstraction is one colour per borough (Fig. 4.23 p. 141).
3. The grid is the legend. Hennig Table 3.5 (pp. 101-102): the original grid drawn through the transformation
   "explains where the physical space has been distorted to what extent"; line weight balanced so other
   layers stay visible; no scale bar anywhere in the book. Graticule only on non-population maps (p. 100).
   Vignelli's subway map rules an A-J / 1-10 index grid over transformed space (Kriz p. 37).
4. The ocean carries the transform. The Atlas keeps a pale ocean with a faint graticule that goes from straight
   (p. 20) to swooping curves (p. 94); Hennig's gridded world floats in white with the continents apart
   (p. 113). Nothing is deleted: shrunken territories become hairline filaments in place (Atlas pp. 158, 195).
5. The caption is the reading rule. Atlas: "The size of each territory indicates ..." as the second line of
   every map; Phaidon captions open with the claim ("the larger a country, the more poor people it has",
   p. 97); Strange Maps explains by named equalities (Indonesia holds eleven Australias, p. 192) and by
   country outlines at true relative area as the unit (p. 186): a Belgium beats a humeter.
6. Restraint. Two inks, one accent, ocean as paper or a neutral tint, blue only when water is the point
   (Phaidon pp. 196, 235); extrinsic complexity overwhelms non-experts (Kriz p. 126).

## What this means for us

- Our pure frame (ocean 5%) removes exactly the device every book relies on: the gaps between continents. The
  metric on land does not depend on the ocean share, so a 15-25% ocean costs nothing in purity and buys the
  continents back. The hero still should use it; the pure frame stays as the "no ocean at all" variant.
- Regional palette (UN subregions, lightness steps within), the ordinary graticule stretched, a reference inset
  in the same colours, a people-square legend, a one-line reading rule and a line of named equalities.
- Coastlines drawn as polylines through the warp overshoot in folded seams (the black scribbles in every render
  so far); draw them as edges of the warped fill instead, at constant width in the output.
- Four hero pieces, one visual language: the today world; the morph loop geography to cartogram; time as eight
  small multiples; the GDP pair with GDP per person-year painted as the ratio.
